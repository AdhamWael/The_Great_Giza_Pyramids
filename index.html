Welcome to the cradle of civilization, where the sands of time have preserved the greatest wonders of the ancient world.

From the majestic Pyramids of Giza to the silent Sphinx, every block of stone tells a story of ambition, engineering, and divinity.

Rising above the West Bank of the Nile, the Giza Plateau served as the necropolis for the Old Kingdom's Fourth Dynasty. This sacred ground was meticulously planned, reflecting the Egyptians' deep understanding of astronomy, geometry, and the afterlife. It was not merely a cemetery, but a grand architectural manifesto of eternal life.

The Giza Plateau

The pyramids of ancient Egypt are among the most famous structures in the world. Built during the Old Kingdom, they served as tombs for powerful pharaohs and reflected the Egyptians’ strong belief in the afterlife. The three main pyramids on the Giza Plateau—those of Khufu, Khafre, and Menkaure—are the most significant examples of this architectural achievement.

Khufu: The Great Pyramid

Constructed for the Pharaoh Khufu around 2560 BC, the Great Pyramid is a technical marvel. Standing at an original height of 146 meters, it was the tallest man-made structure in the world for over 3,800 years. Consisting of approximately 2.3 million stone blocks, each weighing several tons, it remains the last standing wonder of the ancient world.

Khafre: The Horizon

Built by Khufu's son, the Pyramid of Khafre is the second largest in Giza. It is often distinguished by the remains of the original polished limestone casing at its summit. This monument is also famous for its association with the Great Sphinx, the colossal guardian with a lion's body and a pharaoh's head, which stands watch at the entrance of the valley.

Menkaure: The Divine Smallest

The Pyramid of Menkaure, built for the grandson of Khufu, is the smallest of the three major pyramids. Though smaller in scale, its lower courses were encased in costly red granite, shipped from Aswan. Its interior chambers reveals some of the most intricate and well-preserved granite work in the entire complex, representing a transition towards more refined architectural finesse.
